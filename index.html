The Bushfire Crisis In Australia

Bushfires in Australia have always been a serious issue. The so-called "Lucky Country" has endured some of the worst fire periods experienced by any nation on earth, earning names such as The Black Summer in 2019 and being known at times as the Country on Fire. But what is the true reason behind why Australia burns so frequently? Is there a pattern? How does it compare to the rest of the globe? And most importantly, are we getting better?

The Australian Impact & The Black Summer

Australia is known globally for its devastating fires, but just how bad are they, and where do they hit hardest? The following explores when Australia has experienced the most destruction, and more specifically, where.

This choropleth map shows the total number of burns per state, normalised per 10,000 square kilometres, across the five summer seasons from 2016 to 2021. The data reveals which parts of Australia are most affected, typically those closer to the equator. The map also highlights the dramatically elevated figures during the 2019-20 season, widely known as Black Summer. Due to normalisation by area, the ACT appears disproportionately high, indicating that relative to its size it experiences a significant number of burns.

The bump chart compares raw total burn counts, not normalised, to show which state leads each season by volume. It is clear that the Northern Territory and Queensland are the most heavily impacted, while the Australian Capital Territory consistently records the fewest burns.

This line chart shows the total area burnt across Australia each year. There is a clear peak in 2019 at approximately 38 million hectares, followed by a sharp decline to around 19 million in 2020. This drop reflects the increased investment in recovery and fire management techniques implemented in the aftermath of Black Summer.

This grouped bar chart shows the number of forest areas burnt across each fire season from 2016 to 2021, broken down by land type. Native Forest consistently dominates across all seasons, peaking in 2019-20 during Black Summer with 17,033 recorded burns. Commercial Plantation, Non Forest and Other Forest remain comparatively minimal throughout, highlighting that Australia's native bushland bears the overwhelming burden of fire damage.

This map highlights bushfires detected from space with 100% confidence, coloured by brightness as a proxy for fire intensity. It identifies which areas of Australia experienced the most intense fires during the peak 2019 fire season. A concentration of fires is visible across the northern regions and eastern coastline, which aligns with the state-level data shown above. The time brush allows the viewer to filter by date, pinpointing which days saw the most severe activity and where those fires were located.

Fire Area Types

We know Australia burns frequently, but is there a pattern to what burns? The following explores which land types are most vulnerable and whether repeat burning is a growing concern.

This stacked bar chart shows the total area burnt in each land type across Australia per year, measured in millions of hectares. Since roughly 1996 there has been a clear dominance of arid rangelands and savanna woodlands over temperate forests, reflecting the terrain typical of rural areas such as the Northern Territory.

This bar chart shows how many forest areas have experienced unplanned burns across multiple seasons. Most areas have only burnt once, occurring 4,903 times, however repeat burning is far from rare. Notably, 85 forest areas have been burnt five times across the recorded seasons, raising concerns about long-term ecological recovery.

This line chart compares the total number of planned versus unplanned burns across fire seasons. In three of the five years recorded, unplanned fires outnumbered planned ones, reinforcing just how unpredictable and difficult to manage Australian bushfires remain.

Australia against the Globe

The scale of Australia's fire problem is clear. But how does it compare to the rest of the world?

(Click on a region to view its countries) These charts show the number of NASA-identified at-risk fire locations by region, and then by individual country within each region. South Asia accounts for the largest share with 20.6% of all locations, while Australia has the smallest regional share at just 5.8%. However, when broken down by country, Australia stands out dramatically, recording 900 locations, significantly more than the next highest, Sri Lanka, with 662. This suggests that while the Australian region is small, fire risk is heavily concentrated within the country itself.

(Enter a different country to view their position.)

This scatter plot, defaulted to highlight Australia, compares every country in the world on total fire count and average area burnt per fire in 2025. Australia ranks 12th for total fires and 4th for average area lost per fire, confirming that while Australia may not have the most fires globally, those it does experience are among the largest and most destructive on earth.

Conclusion

The data paints a complex picture. Australia's fires have always been severe, but the Black Summer of 2019-20 represented a watershed moment, burning tens of millions of hectares and forcing a national reckoning with how we manage fire. In the years since, the numbers have improved. Area burnt has dropped significantly, and the balance between planned and unplanned burns has shifted. Yet the underlying vulnerability remains. Australia consistently ranks among the worst affected nations on earth, not just in frequency but in the sheer scale of individual fires. NASA data confirms that fire risk is heavily concentrated within Australia compared to every other country in its region. Climate, terrain and land type all conspire to make this country uniquely exposed. The answer to whether we are getting better is cautiously yes, but the work is far from done. The data shows progress, but it also shows how quickly conditions can turn. Understanding where fires burn, what burns, and why is not just an academic exercise, it is the foundation of every decision that will determine how the next Black Summer unfolds.
